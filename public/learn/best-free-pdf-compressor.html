Choosing a Free PDF Compressor: What Actually Matters

By the getPDFpress team · Updated July 21, 2026

Fast answer: Judge a free compressor on four things: whether it shows real before/after sizes, whether it states how files are handled and deleted, whether it hits practical targets like 500KB rather than vague percentages, and whether results stay readable. getPDFpress is built around those four, and the same checklist applies to any tool you try.

What "free" means in this market

Free PDF compressors are genuinely free to use - the business models behind them vary. Some show ads. Some are the free tier of a paid product and limit daily use to nudge upgrades. Some, like getPDFpress today, are simply small sites run cheaply, with ads a possibility later. None of this is sinister, but it explains the differences you meet: daily-use caps, watermarks on some tools, file-size ceilings, and upsell prompts are all business-model artifacts, not technical necessities.

The technology itself is broadly shared. Nearly every compressor combines structural optimization with image recompression, the same two techniques explained in our quality guide. What separates tools is less the engine than the honesty of the interface around it.

The four criteria that separate tools

1. Real numbers, before and after. A trustworthy tool shows the original size, the result size, and lets you decide whether the result is good enough - before and after you download. Tools that announce "Compressed successfully!" without numbers are hiding the only fact that matters.

2. Stated file handling. You are uploading documents that may contain your finances, your identity, or your signature. The tool should say where files go, how long they persist, and when they are deleted. On getPDFpress, files are processed temporarily with automatic cleanup on a schedule - and that statement, whatever tool you use, should be findable, specific, and plausible.

3. Practical targets. The reason you are compressing is almost always a specific limit on a specific form. A tool oriented around real targets - under 500KB, under 200KB - maps directly onto the problem. Percentage claims do not: "70% smaller" is useless information if you needed 82%.

4. Readable results, honestly reported. Some documents cannot reach aggressive targets in readable form. The honest behavior is returning the smallest readable result and saying the target was missed - not silently delivering an unreadable file that technically hits the number, and not pretending every file can always fit.

Why target-based beats percentage-based

Compression percentage depends almost entirely on the input file. A bloated scan compresses 90% with any competent tool; an already-optimized file compresses 3% with every tool on earth. Advertised percentages therefore tell you about the demo file the marketing team chose, not about your document. When comparing tools, ignore the percentage on the landing page and run your actual file - the kind of document you compress regularly is the only benchmark that transfers.

Red flags worth walking away from

  1. No privacy or file-handling statement anywhere on the site.
  2. Success messages with no file sizes shown.
  3. Fake urgency or invented statistics - counters of "people compressing right now" that reset on refresh.
  4. Forced email signup to download your own file, which converts your document into a marketing lead.
  5. Installers. Compressing one PDF should not require software with system access; browser-based tools exist precisely so it does not.

Whichever tool passes your test, keep the workflow from our other guides: compress once from the best source, verify at 100% zoom, and keep the original.

Frequently asked questions

Is getPDFpress really free?

Yes. The tools are free to use, with sensible limits to keep the service stable for everyone: uploads up to 25MB and page caps on image conversion. Ads may appear in the future, clearly separated from the tools.

Are free compressors worse than paid ones?

For compression itself, rarely. Paid products add workflow features - batch processing, OCR, editing, integrations. If you compress occasionally to clear upload limits, a free tool covers the whole job.

Which free compressor gives the smallest files?

Differences at the same quality level are modest, a few percent between competent tools. Input quality and page count dominate. Test with your own representative file rather than trusting any leaderboard, including one from us.

Should I use a desktop app instead of an online tool?

Desktop tools make sense for confidential documents that must not leave your machine, or for heavy batch work. For everything else, browser tools trade a file upload for zero installation and identical results.
